## Abstract

Sentencing decisions are the product of a group of courtroom actors, primarily judges and district attorneys. Although the structure of the courtroom workgroup and the interdependencies among members are assumed to be important determinants of sentencing decisions, the degree of this importance and the specific mechanisms through which workgroups affect these decisions have not been investigated. This study used data from the Pennsylvania Commission on Sentencing (PCS) for the years 1990 to 2000 to examine how three social psychological aspects of courtroom workgroups (similarity, proximity, and stability) affect sentencing decisions. Results indicated (a) that workgroups generally had very high levels of similarity in terms of race, gender, and political party but lower levels of similarity in terms of age, college education, and law school education and (b) that proximity and stability were generally high. Controlling for individual, case, and distal contextual factors, workgroup factors affected the decision to incarcerate, the decision to impose fines, and the decision to impose restitution. In particular, proximity increased the use of economic sanctions relative to incarceration and stability was associated with a decrease in the imposition of economic sanctions. Similarity had inconsistent effects.

## Research Questions

**Focus is on the district attorney for a particular county and all judges in that county**.

* Similarity --> The degree to which work group members share the same characteristics.
	* Race/gender/age.
	* College and law school education (attended school in PA or not).
	* Political beliefs.
* Proximity --> Location of work group members' officers in relation to one another.
	* Organizational settings provide opportunities for individuals to form interpersonal ties. The geography of the court house (e.g., availability of informal meeting places) structures court communities and opportunities for interaction.
* Stability --> The number of years work group members worked together in the same jurisdiction.
	* Creates familiarity and patterns of communication. Reduces uncertainty about others' intentions and probable behaviors.

From the original [[eisensteinContoursJusticeCommunities1988]] and [[eisensteinFelonyJusticeOrganizational1977]] studies, they hypothesized:

1) Degree of familiarity.
2) Context in which the court is located.
3) County legal culture (i.e., work group members' shared beliefs about interpersonal relationships and the manner in which cases should be disposed of).
## Data

### Dependent variable

Differing types, severity, and discretion.
1) Decision to incarcerate.
2) Imposition of fines.
3) Imposition of restitution.
### Independent variables

**County level**
1) % living in an urban area.
2) % living in poverty.
3) % who voted Republican.
4) Part I crime offenses per 100,000.
5) % male.
6) **Similarity**:
	1) Measured as whether or not the individual shared the same value for the key variable. For age, was the district attorney and the judge within 5 years of age of each other?
	2) Measured at the county level so it captured e.g., the % of judges who shared the same race as the district attorney in that year. Then averaged across the 11 years to come up with a value for that county.
7) **Proximity**
	1) Did the president judge (administrative head of the county court elected by all of the judges) and DA have offices in the same building?
8) **Stability**
	1) The number of years the judge and district attorney had worked together in the same jurisdiction. In each county and each year, aggregated across all judges. Then averaged across all years to come up with a county-level measure.

**Case level**
1) Prior record --> Categorical.
2) Offense severity (only include most serious offense)
3) Type of offense (i.e., person, property, drug, traffic, other)
4) Disposition (i.e., jury trial, bench trial, plea)
5) Type of sentence (i.e., prison, jail, probation)
6) Fines (0/1)
7) Restitution (0/1)
8) Offender race/gender/age

Excluded all but an offender's first offense in a year. Final sample size is 549,942 cases.

## Results

* Offenders were more likely to be incarcerated with counties with: **1)** Less gender similarity, **2)** Less age similarity, **3)** Less law school similarity, **4)** More college similarity, and **5)** Less political party similarity. --> **At least for law school, similar professionalization affects later sentencing practices.** On the other hand, **the college finding is a bit perplexing. More ties to local communities and punitive beliefs? Since it is coded as % who attended Pennsylvania colleges. Less exposure to diversity?**
* More stable work groups were less likely to impose fines. More proximal work groups were more likely to impose fines. Counties with higher college similarity were more likely to impose fines. **Strongest effects, judges have most discretion? Guidelines less influential here.** 
* Higher law school similarity decreased the odds of receiving an order of restitution. Stability significant in some model specifications (associated with lower odds of restitution).
* Stability did not seem significantly related to whether or not the statutory change led to an actual change in practices.
## Conclusions

* Above and beyond case-characteristics, work group factors did affect outcomes.
* Many of the county *distal factors* (e.g., poverty) did not affect the outcomes. Perhaps they are mediated by the courtroom variables?
* Not a lot of variation in some of the courtroom work group variables (e.g., proximity, race, gender, schooling) perhaps explaining some of the insignificant findings.
* Found these results in a [[Sentencing guidelines|sentencing guidelines]] state. Might results be different where discretion is less limited?
* **Proximity** --> Led to a lower odds of incarceration (although not significant) but also higher likelihood of imposing fines. When courtroom actors are together, they are more likely to impose a trade-off in economic vs. non-economic sanctions?
* **Stability** --> Less likely to impose fines and restitution. More familiar with offender inability to pay? Significant in the post-statutory change model so suggests some resistance to complying with the new rule? Resistant to change?
* Work group characteristics operated similarly across all counties.
