## Abstract

Although recent studies have suggested that social media sites play a crucial role in amplifying far-right ideas, the exact mechanisms by which this process occurs remain unclear. To address this gap, I utilize data from life history interviews with 55 far-right youth activists involved in right-wing campus organizations in a major city in central Canada and another in the Midwestern United States. I found that, during interviews, far-right participants made sense of their offline mobilization and involvement in right-wing organizations through their exposure to far-right content during adolescence. Participants recounted their experiences viewing far-right YouTube content, describing how they: 1) developed parasocial relationships with those on the far-right, 2) became politically polarized, and 3) were inspired by online content to seek offline collective mobilization at university. This study examines broader shifts in political engagement and the impact of technology on the future of democracies.

## Notes

* What factors contribute to far-right youth engaging in offline mobilization at university campuses?
  
	* Parasocial relationships formed during adolescence with far-right influencers on platforms like YouTube played a central role in shaping participants' political identities. Once they entered university, they sought formal organizations in which to enact their identity offline. Essentially, once these individuals entered college, they were able to leverage their newfound freedom and abundant free time to become active in right-wing clubs. These clubs validated the things they believed/watched in their youth. Led to the formation of collective or group identities. Essentially, like-minded individuals coming together to form cognitive, emotional, and social connections.
	  
	* However, not only was an in-group identity formed, but the participants recounted how they also learned to dislike/hate *the left* and to view them as the enemy contributing to [[affective polarization]].
	  
	* This is contrary to prior research which has posited that a person's family and/or friends which typically expose them to online social movements and help build their political identity.
	  
	* At least among study participants, identification with the right-wing superseded other aspects of identity like race and sex.
	  
	* An interesting note is that none of the participants had joined an offline right-wing organization prior to university.

## Thoughts

* While the author does claim they cannot draw conclusive causal conclusions, they sometimes make statements hinting at it. For example, "All participants confirmed that it was not family or friends who first introduced them to far-right online content, but that it appeared in their social media feeds unprompted."
  
* This is what I view as the main limitation of the study. What is the causal power of right-wing influencers? Are social media algorithms causing the youth to become more right-leaning?
  
	* It's possible that a low % of people exposed to right-wing influencers actually have their views changed, but when looking at current individuals who identify as right-wing, a large % of them got to those views through online content. The question becomes, though, would these people have become right-wing anyway?
