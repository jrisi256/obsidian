## Abstract

Investigations of how criminal justice actors contribute to variation in sentencing typically focus on the role played by the judge. We argue that sentencing should be viewed as a collaborative process involving actors other than the judge and that the role of the prosecutor is particularly salient. We also contend that the courtroom workgroup literature has suggested that sentences may vary depending on the particular judge and prosecutor to whom the case is assigned. By using a unique data set from three U.S. district courts (N = 2,686) that identifies both the judge and the prosecutor handling the case, we examine how the judge, the prosecutor, and the judge–prosecutor dyad contribute to variance in offender sentences. We do this by employing cross-classified random-effects models to estimate the variance components associated with judges, prosecutors, and judge–prosecutor interactions. The results indicate that disparity attributable to the prosecutor is larger than disparity from the judge. Moreover, the role that the judge plays is moderated by the prosecutor to whom the case is assigned, as the judge–prosecutor effect is consistently larger than other random effects across the models. We also find that results vary by judicial district.

## Overview

Don’t focus so much on judges. Sentencing is a collaborative process involving other actors, importantly the prosecutor. Having a unique dataset identifying judges, prosecutors, and the dyad and use this to see how individuals contribute to variance in decisions. Results indicate disparity attributable to the prosecutor is larger than disparity attributable to the judge. The role the judge plays is moderated by the prosecutor to whom the case is assigned.
## Introduction

Really hones in on how much discretion prosecutors have and leans into the idea that sentencing guidelines may have just shifted discretion to prosecutors. Thus looking at whether or not sentencing guidelines have reduced disparities for judges is misleading.
## Past Research
  
Really inconclusive results as to whether or not the federal guidelines actually decreased disparity. One interesting study looked at judges’ decisions before/after (the same judges) as well as federal judicial districts before/after and found “modest success in reducing inter-judge disparity” for some offenses and some districts. Other studies come to different, more negative conclusions. All in all, interjudge disparity in state and federal courts is still alive and suggests there is nontrivial variation. Constraining judicial discretion hasn’t eliminated it. However none of these studies consider the prosecutor.

**Hydraulic displacement of discretion hypothesis** --> Research is limited and mixed. Hasn’t found much support. However some research has shown prosecutors are important and confirms the criticism of those who criticized sentencing reforms for ignoring prosecutors.
## Research Questions

1. **Is there interjudge disparity in sentencing net of the effects of the prosecutor and controls for the offender and the case?** Evidence suggests there is interjudge disparity. In contrast to the other groups, puzzlingly found that disparities increased when including controls.
2. **Is there inter-prosecutor disparity in sentencing net of the judge and offender/case characteristics?** There is some disparity, but it seems as if most of the disparity arises from differences in case composition.
3. **Do sentences vary depending on the judge-prosecutor dyad? Net of other stuff.** Sentences appear to vary based on the particular combination of judge and prosecutor, above and beyond judge/prosecutor main effects. Even higher variance than that from individual judges or prosecutors. Including individual/case-level controls reduced disparities quite a bit.
4. **Do the effects of judge, prosecutor, and dyads vary across district courts?** Findings suggest the amount of disparity varies (by quite a bit in some cases) in each of the 3 districts investigated.
## Data

Three U.S. district courts. Identifies actors. 2,686 offenders nested within 18 judges, 97 prosecutors, and 431 dyads. Dependent Variable: Sentence length.

## Methodology

Natural experiments [[judge instrumental variables]] and HLMs suffer from several limitations. Both fail to consider the role of prosecutors. Even random assignment of judges doesn’t completely filter out the role prosecutors can play because they’re still influencing cases as they’re randomly assigned to judges (I guess the argument here is there is an imbalance in the covariates?) Perhaps more convincingly they can argue judge behavior changes as a result of exposure to some prosecutor. (but even here shouldn’t this be balanced because of random assignment with a large enough sample).

So they use cross-classified multilevel modelling which accounts for their theoretical insights. Figure 1 is very useful for illustrating the dynamic. Very interesting.
## Results

1. Overall amount of variation in sentence length ranged from 6 months to 1 year generally. Prosecutorial variance was higher than that of judges. However most of the disparity seems to arise from differences in offender and case characteristics (not from differences in the judge or prosecutor handling the case). This isn’t surprising given the rigidity of the federal sentencing guidelines.
2. Why the difference in outcomes when including controls? Judges are assigned cases randomly and will probably interpret the circumstances of the cases differently. Prosecutors aren’t assigned cases randomly. They specialize in case types.
3. “The fact there is relatively little sentencing disparity attributable to the judge… but substantial disparity attributable to the prosecutor and to the combination of a particular judge with a particular prosecutor suggests sentence disparity results primarily but not exclusively from the exercise of prosecutorial discretion.”
4. Cannot compare pre and post guideline discretion and thus cannot say whether or not disparities increased/decreased after the guideline implementation. Contradicts statement they just made about the federal sentencing guidelines shifting discretion from judges to prosecutors. “... broad discretion available to prosecutors under the guidelines coupled with the constraints on judicial discretion, has compromised the uniformity and consistency in sentencing the guidelines were designed to produce."
