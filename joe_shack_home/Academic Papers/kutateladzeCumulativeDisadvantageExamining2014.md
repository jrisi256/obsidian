## Abstract

Current research on criminal case processing typically examines a single decision-making point, so drawing reliable conclusions about the impact that factors such as defendants' race or ethnicity exert across successive stages of the justice system is difficult. Using data from the New York County District Attorney's Office that tracks 185,275 diverse criminal cases, this study assesses racial and ethnic disparity for multiple discretionary points of prosecution and sentencing. Findings from multivariate logistic regression analyses demonstrate that the effects of race and ethnicity vary by discretionary point and offense category. Black and Latino defendants were more likely than White defendants to be detained, to receive a custodial plea offer, and to be incarcerated—and they received especially punitive outcomes for person offenses—but were more likely to benefit from case dismissals. The findings for Asian defendants were less consistent but suggest they were the least likely to be detained, to receive custodial offers, and to be incarcerated. These findings are discussed in the context of contemporary theoretical perspectives on racial bias and cumulative disadvantage in the justice system.

- **What is the question?** What is the cumulative impact of race across successive stages of the criminal justice system?
	- Assess racial/ethnic disparity across multiple discretionary points of prosecuting and sentencing.
	- **H1** --> Black and Latino defendants will be more likely than similarly situated White defendants to experience outcome-specific disadvantages at individual stages of criminal case processing.
	- **H2** → Same but for cumulative disadvantages across combinations of more criminal case-processing outcomes.
	- **H3** → Asian defendants will not experience similar outcome-specific or cumulative disadvantages as Black/Latino defendants.
	- **H4** → Inclusion of socioeconomic proxies will reduce the effects of race/ethnicity on outcome-specific and cumulative disadvantages in criminal case processing. Did reduce effects but the effect of race remained significant.

- **Why is it important?** Studies which only look at one stage may miss how cumulative disadvantage has accrued and determine there’s no evidence of bias. Additionally bias in one stage may be offset in another stage. We need a better understanding of how race gets processed through all stages of the CJ system.
    
- **How do you propose to answer it?** Using data from the New York County DA’s office which has information on 185,275 criminal cases.
	- Use proxies for social class (type of defense attorney and neighborhood arrest location) → the idea being more affluent defendants will have private attorneys. **Very questionable**.
	- **Dependent Variables** → **1)** Does the DA decide to file charges? Case Acceptance **2)** Pretrial detention, **3)** Case dismissal, is the case dismissed by the judge or prosecutor at any point from here on out, **4)** Custodial plea offer, whether defendants receive custodial sentences (i.e. an offer for a prison sentence/jail) or not, **5)** Incarceration sentence, was the individual incarcerated? 
	- Use [[Heckman’s correction]] to control for [[selection bias]] caused by previous decision points. When the temporal ordering is clear, they include that variable as a control in the model (e.g. pretrial detention).
	- Ran separate models for different types of offenses.
	- To investigate cumulative racial disadvantage, calculate predicted probabilities from the regression models which predict membership in different combinations of outcomes (e.g. being detained, not dismissed, and incarcerated).

- **What do you find?** The effects of race vary by discretionary point and offense category.
	- Black/Latino defendants were more likely to be detained, to receive a custodial plea offer, and to be incarcerated. Especially penalized for person-based violent offenses. **Evidence of cumulative disadvantage too**.
		- Interestingly had higher odds of case dismissal vs. White defendants. Is this leniency? Or a mechanism for declining cases which should have been rejected at initial screening? Police more likely to arrest minorities with less evidence? **Highly speculative**.
	- Findings for Asian defendants were less consistent. Suggest they were the least likely to be detained, to receive custodial offers, and to be incarcerated.
