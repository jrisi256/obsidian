## Abstract

The federal criminal justice system applies a single set of federal statutes and identical rules of procedure in a variety of local district jurisdictions. It is an underexplored research setting rich with potential to advance our theoretical understanding of how important sociological and political processes work. In particular, the sentencing and case processing practices of federal district courts are governed by the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines, which are in part supposed to impose uniformity and predictability in federal criminal sentencing. Guided by a processual order perspective that views courts as communities with their own distinct organizational culture, practices, and relationships, I explore the localized meaning and uses of key provisions of the sentencing guidelines, such as guideline departures for “substantial assistance” to law enforcement, sentence reductions for “acceptance of responsibility,” and the role of federal probation presentence reports. Data for this study come from field interviews with federal prosecutors, judges, public and private defense attorneys, and probation officers in four U.S. District Courts. I supplement these interview data with quantitative sentencing data that show substantial variation in criminal punishment between these four courts. I argue that federal criminal courts, like state courts, are best conceptualized as communities with distinctive processual orders.

**Key Question**: Who controls the use and interpretation of the federal sentencing guidelines? The answer from this research paper is that it’s largely local federal court actors. Federal trial courts are best seen as social worlds then with their own distinct processual orders.

**Introduction**: On the one hand, there are many factors in place which might suggest great uniformity amongst federal courts. E.g. all federal attorneys are appointed by the president and are then responsible to the Attorney General. The DOJ establishes uniform policy. On paper, there is a great deal of centralization of power. Paper argues the opposite of course.  

**Processual Order Perspective**: Favors the view federal district courts vary considerably in their interpretation and implementation of federal law and sentencing policy. Human agency and interaction are very important in organizations aside from their formal structure and rules. Social worlds -> networks of participation in regular activity and mutual response.
- Interaction processes and resulting outcomes are crucial to the development of institutional organization.
- Various interaction processes (negotiation, persuasion, manipulation) will have different salience and be of greater or less significance for particular instances of any social order.
- Processual orders are embedded in social worlds which set opportunities for and constraints on various alternative action strategies. 
- Processual orders exhibit norms and conventions which are accumulations of past problem-solving interaction processes and stay that way until new problems emerge which cannot be resolved using the old ways.
    
Courts are specific instantiations of social worlds. Going rates, plea agendas (represents typical set of bargaining chips or concessions on the table as part of the plea).

5k1 departure is when a defendant gets a downward departure for offering substantial assistance to law enforcement.

**Interviews**: Use and interpretation of five key features of the USA Sentencing Guidelines vary.
- **Relevant conduct** (sentencing guidelines are very technical in how to create a sentence for someone based on the role they played, amount of harm caused, etc.), **Substantial assistance** (downward departures can be recommended for those who help the police), **Acceptance of Responsibility** (downward departure for those who accept responsibility). **Pre-sentence Reports** (Probation Office calculates defendant’s sentence range, Probation Office becomes the keeper of the sentencing guidelines), **Re-sentencing** (If the prosecution petitions the court w/in one year of the sentence, the court may reduce the sentence to reflect a defendant’s substantial assistance. Also known as Rule 35.
- All the districts varied based on the interviews provided on how they viewed each of these 5 key features.
- Judges would defer to the plea agreement over the recommendations of the pre-sentence report. Thought probation officers were not lawyers and thus were not stewards of the law. Held less weight as a result when it came to the crafting of sentences. **BUT** this differed and changed from district to district. Some judges sided with the probation office or at least it was more common in certain districts vs. other districts (**speculate that the one district hired attorneys as probation officers likely leading to the effect they were respected more**).
    
**Quantitative Results** also suggest a different weighing of factors on likelihood of incarceration and subsequent sentence length and likelihood of departures. Also extralegal effects, “...variation in federal court communities’ processual orders of case processing and sentencing may be related to racial, ethnic, and gender disparities in federal criminal punishment.”

**Conclusion**: “... evidence of localized court community processual orders even in the face of potentially strong meta-power processes that would encourage centralization and uniformity…”

On the one hand, the Sentencing Guidelines heavily structure the plea agenda in each district. Negotiations revolve around what is or isn’t offense-relevant conduct, what is or isn’t substantial assistance, what is or isn’t acceptance of responsibility. But then on the other hand, there’s enormous discretion and leeway in terms of how these things get interpreted.

Next steps are to connect processual orders to characteristics of these court communities. Social, political, and cultural contexts of the surrounding community, relationships, power dynamics, ideologies, size, stability of membership.
