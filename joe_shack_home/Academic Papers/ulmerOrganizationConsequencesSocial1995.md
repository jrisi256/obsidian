## Abstract

This article demonstrates the contributions of Katovich and Couch's (1992) formal theory of social pasts to conceptualizations of social world contexts and processual order (Strauss 1978; 1993). Using interview and observational data from three criminal courts, I focus on the interrelationship of two social world contextual features: (1) the organization of participants' social pasts, and (2) the degree of ideological consensus between participants in terms of sentencing goals. These two factors interact to influence interorganizational relationships in the three courts; and, in combination with individual criminal case characteristics, influence actors' case processing strategies. The article concludes by discussing the implications of the organization of social pasts in criminal courts and organizational arenas in general for the availability and attractiveness of alternative action strategies, as well as the management of uncertainty in organizational decision making. I also suggest several directions for further research.

**Katovich and Couch**
* Distinguish between shared and common pasts. Important for identity building in individuals and for organizations. Shared pasts are when actors have engaged with each other before. When you have a shared past, you can quickly place each other based on inferred functional identities. Also allows for prediction and managing uncertainty which comes with the future.
  
* Common pasts reflect the independent experience of similar joint acts in different contexts. This involves placing people based on inferred cat]egories (not developed through direct interpersonal communication). Useful when you don’t have a shared past, but you all have participated in similar activities just in different settings with different people. Also used to manage uncertainty.

**Strauss**
* Processual Order Theory’s conceptualization of the temporal dimensions of social worlds --> trajectory and biography --> emphasizes embeddedness of social interactions in multilayered contexts (which includes the temporal context). We can augment this theory with notions of shared and common pasts. We consider criminal courts as social worlds where individuals with social pasts with each other develop relationships, shared perspectives, stocks of knowledge, and routinized actions.
  
* Previous research suggests courtrooms with robust shared pasts (membership is more stable and members are more familiar) will have stronger informal case processing and sentencing norms. Plea bargaining will be easier and more cooperative. Less workgroup familiarity fosters unilateral decision making. However ideological conflict presents a potential mediating variable to the robustness of the shared past.

**Current Research**: Look at the organization of social pasts and the degree of consensus between participants regarding sentencing ideologies. And then how they interact with characteristics of individual cases. Corresponds to 1) Temporality of interaction (social pasts), 2) Ideologies and perspectives of participants, 3) Complexity of issues involved.

**Social Pasts**: The three court communities differed in the robustness of their shared pasts as indicated by membership stability and familiarity of participants. Some places everybody knew everybody (interpersonal familiarity). In other places there was much higher turnover and less face-to-face time not allowing for such interpersonal familiarity and shared pasts to develop. Could rely on the common past when there was no shared past (**lacking robust shared pasts and accompanying traditions and norms, relied on common past of working sentencing guidelines and dealing with diverse styles and preferences of judges**).

**Sentencing Ideologies**: Again the three court communities differed concerning the degree of ideological consistency present. When courtroom actors know everyone shares the same goals, it can make things run smoother. People were more likely to believe in rehabilitation (downplay deterrence and incapacitation) or vice versa. Particularly in the Republican county, it was kind of like a game. You couldn’t ruffle too many feathers, and you had to be really careful to pick and choose your battles.

- Importantly, robust shared pasts of ideological conflict have different outcomes vs. ideological harmony (cooperation and collegiality). Can be used to mobilize conflictive strategies and coalitions. Really interesting quote on pg. 13 -14.

**Case Processing**: Social pasts and interorganizational relations color the dominant stocks of knowledge used for uncertainty reduction, processing routine AND problematic cases, and establishing going rates. Definitions of individual cases also provides a third important contextual dimension. Generally how routine or problematic a case was depended on strength of evidence, severity of offense, perceptions of defendant characteristics. **Sometimes these local dynamics for going rates were so strong they superseded state sentencing guidelines**.
